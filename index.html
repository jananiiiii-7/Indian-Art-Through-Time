FOLLOW THE MAHABHARATA

How Indian Artists Interpreted the Epic Across 1,500 Years

Historical Note: The Mahabharata is one of humanity's greatest epic literary traditions. This gallery explores how artists in different centuries, mediums, and courtly regions visually interpreted the epic. We clearly distinguish sacred mythological narrative traditions from verifiable archaeological evidence.

From 6th-century Gupta stone reliefs at Deogarh to open-air Pallava rock carvings in Tamil Nadu, Akbar’s royal Mughal manuscript prints, Mewar Rajput miniatures, and Raja Ravi Varma's Victorian-style oils—discover how the Mahabharata served as a timeless canvas for artists exploring dharma, duty, victory, and human emotion.

ART-HISTORICAL ANALYSIS: WHAT CHANGED & WHAT STAYED THE SAME?

Medium & Scale:

Pallava sculpture utilizes massive public open-air granite boulders carved in high relief, whereas Mughal art uses intimate, portable paper folios painted with fine squirrel-hair brushes for royal library viewing.

Regional Style:

The Pallava rock relief incorporates South Indian naturalism and ascetics, while the Mughal miniature combines Persian high-horizon landscape conventions with lively Indian narrative crowds.

Patronage & Purpose:

Pallava kings carved monumentality to affirm divine royal cosmic duty, while Akbar commissioned the *Razmnama* to foster interfaith synthesis and Persian scholarly understanding.
